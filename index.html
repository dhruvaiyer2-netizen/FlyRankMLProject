Which well-ranked pages under-capture clicks?

A baseline-first, leakage-checked scoring study on FlyRank search data (March–April 2026)

Abstract

We asked which well-ranked pages earn fewer clicks than their search position suggests, and whether a model can predict which ones will keep doing so. Using page-level search data for 55 anonymized clients (61,924 March 2026 pages with at least 500 impressions), we built a transparent rule that scores each page by how far its click-through rate (CTR) sits below the median for its position tier, and treated it as the baseline. We compared it with logistic regression and a Random Forest that use March data only to predict April underperformance, under client-grouped and time-aware validation with leakage checks. The rule alone reaches ROC-AUC of about 0.82, the forest's measured gain over it is small (about +0.006 to +0.009 under the stricter designs), and its precision among the top 500 pages is about 0.93–0.94 vs 0.87–0.88 for the rule, against a base rate near 0.52. The output is a ranked review queue for human decision-support, not evidence that any edit will raise clicks.

1. Problem framing

Decision. Which already-visible pages should an editor open first to check the title, description, and match to search intent?

Unit and output. One row is one page over one calendar month. The output is a score, one reason code (ctr_below_tier_median), and an action label (review_title_meta or monitor), written to a ranked queue.

Action and cost of a wrong call. An editor reviews the top of the queue. A false positive wastes review time or prompts an unneeded edit; a false negative leaves a page under-earning clicks with nobody looking at it.

Why data helps. Thousands of pages are visible, review time is limited, and expected CTR depends strongly on position, so a page must be compared with its own position tier, not with one global number.

2. Data safety

Used. The FlyRank ML Internship warehouse: fact_content_daily_performance (one row per page per client per day; March 2026 has 9,841,378 rows and 9,841,378 unique keys, 55 clients, 331,437 pages) joined to dim_content. Windows were February, March and April 2026. Daily rows were aggregated to page-month with CTR as total clicks over total impressions and position weighted by impressions.

Deliberately excluded.

  • Pages under 500 impressions in the month. With few impressions CTR is nearly all noise: its standard deviation was 8.43 at 1–9 impressions and 0.35 at 500+.
  • FlyRank product outputs (health, priority, action and refresh flags) and label-derived trend fields, so a model cannot learn to copy the product or the label.
  • GA4 engagement fields. Only 413,966 of 9,841,378 March rows (4.2%) have GA4 data.
  • AI-session fields (too sparse) and pages deleted as of the snapshot (2 pages).

Leakage risks considered and checked. Features come from one month and labels from the next, with no overlapping dates (Feb 1–28, Mar 1–31, Apr 1–30). No label or product-flag column was among the features. The strongest single feature reached AUC 0.828 and the rest were 0.50–0.54, so none gives away the label. Training on shuffled labels gave AUC 0.475. Computing tier medians from training pages only moved the rule's AUC from 0.8283 to 0.8276. Hashed identifiers were used for grouping and joins only, never as features, and none are shown here.

Known data gaps. word_count is missing for roughly a quarter of live pages (82% for 2024-created, 40% for 2025, 2.3% for 2026), so a missing flag is included. dim_content is a current snapshot, not as of March.

3. Baseline

Rule. Among pages with at least 500 impressions, group by position tier (1–3, 4–10, 11–20, 21+). Score = tier median CTR minus the page's CTR. A positive score gets the reason code and the review action; the rest are monitored. Ties are broken by impressions, largest first, because zero-click pages share the maximum score and would otherwise be ordered arbitrarily.

Why it is a fair comparison. It is scored on the same pages, the same label, and the same splits and metrics as the models. The tie-break matters: on one test set, precision among the top 500 was 0.820 with arbitrary ties and 0.880 with the impressions tie-break, so comparing the forest only with the arbitrary version would have overstated its edge. Baseline numbers appear with the model numbers in the table in section 5.

4. Model / analysis

Target (proxy, one sentence). For a March page with 500+ impressions in April, the label is 1 if its April CTR is below its April tier median; this is a defined proxy, not an observed outcome of any edit. Of 61,924 qualifying March pages, 51,496 (83.2%) also had 500+ April impressions and received a label.

Features (all knowable at the end of March). March CTR, average position, impressions, the March CTR-gap score, word count and a missing flag, content age at month end, and four search-intent flags.

Left out on purpose. Product flags, GA4 and AI-session fields, any April value, optimization and update dates, and identifiers.

Methods and why. Logistic regression (standardized) as a simple, interpretable comparison, and a Random Forest (minimum leaf size 20) because CTR depends non-linearly on position and volume. Gradient boosting was skipped: it adds tuning risk without a reason to expect a gain on this feature set.

5. Evaluation

Three designs, each repeated over five random seeds: (1) a random split of pages, as a naive reference; (2) a split grouped by client, so test clients are unseen; (3) train on February→March and test on March→April for held-out clients. Metrics are ROC-AUC and precision among the top 500 ranked pages (P@500). The base rate (share of test pages below their next-month tier median) was 0.521 on the main grouped split, so a coin-flip ranking scores about 0.52 at any K.

Logistic regression scored 0.815–0.825 AUC, roughly level with or below the rule. The random split overstated the forest's edge and understated seed-to-seed variation, which is why it is shown only as a reference.

Errors

Overall error was about 24% on the main split. Confident false positives were visible pages with March CTR under 0.1% that recovered in April. Confident false negatives had March CTR of roughly 0.9–2.6% and fell below the median in April, most by a small margin and some on only 700–1,000 impressions. Error also fell with volume: 28% at 500–1,000 impressions, 25% at 1,000–5,000, and 18% above 5,000.

6. Interpretation

What the model leans on. In permutation importance the March CTR gap dominated (AUC drop 0.157), then CTR (0.047), impressions (0.017) and position (0.010). Word count, content age and intent contributed almost nothing. The forest mostly re-learns the rule, which explains its modest gain.

Surprises and negative results. In a small starter sample the 1–3 and 4–10 medians looked identical (0.23); on the full warehouse slice they fall steadily, so the flat pattern was a sample-size artifact. Content metadata added no measurable signal here. Several flagged pages recovered the next month with no known change, so a single-month gap is not a stable diagnosis.

7. Recommendation

Decision-support for human review. Each item says how to check whether it helped.

  1. Review high-visibility zero-click pages first. In tiers 1–3 and 4–10, open pages with the largest CTR gap and most impressions and check the title, description, and match to intent. Check: compare clicks 30 and 60 days later against similar untouched pages.
  2. Prioritise large gaps, monitor small ones. Error was about 10% for the largest March gaps and about 35% for the smallest.
  3. Ignore CTR from pages under 500 impressions. It is dominated by sampling noise.
  4. Re-score monthly and confirm a gap persists before editing.
  5. Use the transparent rule by default; add the model only to rank the top of the queue. Its clearest advantage was P@500 of about 0.93–0.94 vs 0.87–0.88.

Confidence and limits. Confidence is moderate for ordering pages and low for explaining them.

  • Observational, directional results; no causal claim about edits, refreshes, or Google's algorithm.
  • The label is a proxy and covers only pages with 500+ impressions in both months (83% of visible March pages).
  • One or two month transitions, and test sets dominated by a few large clients; other periods may differ.
  • The forest's gain over the rule is small, and a team could reasonably prefer the rule with a tie-break.

8. Reproducibility

Environment. Google Colab (Python 3) with duckdb, huggingface_hub, scikit-learn, pandas and numpy; reading the warehouse needs a Hugging Face token with dataset access.

Re-run from a fresh clone.

git clone https://github.com/dhruvaiyer2-netizen/FlyRankMLProject.git
cd [your-repo]
# open each notebook in Colab (or Jupyter) and Run All, in this order:
#   work/notebooks/w03_data_contract.ipynb
#   work/notebooks/w04_baseline_score.ipynb   (writes work/outputs/baseline_action_score.csv)
#   work/notebooks/w05_model.ipynb
#   work/notebooks/w06_validation_audit.ipynb

Seeds. Models use random_state=42; the Week 6 designs use seeds 0–4 for each split.

Notebooks.

  • w01_research_question.ipynb: lane choice and first numbers
  • w02_ml_task_framing.ipynb: task type and success metric
  • w03_data_contract.ipynb: grain, availability checks, features, leakage trap
  • w04_baseline_score.ipynb: the rule and the ranked queue
  • w05_model.ipynb: models vs baseline
  • w06_validation_audit.ipynb: honest-split re-run and leakage audit

No sealed or blind holdout is claimed.
